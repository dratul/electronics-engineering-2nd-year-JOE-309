This guide covers digital voltmeters and ramp conversion, digital multimeters, analog CRO/oscilloscope fundamentals and measurements, and digital storage oscilloscopes (DSOs).

## Learning objectives

- Explain how a digital voltmeter converts an input voltage to a displayed number.
- Describe ramp-type time measurement and dual-slope conversion.
- Select suitable DMM functions and interpret basic measurement limitations.
- Explain the CRO signal path and calculate voltage, time, frequency, and phase from a display.
- Compare analog oscilloscopes and DSOs and recognize sampling limitations.

## 1. Measurement fundamentals

A measuring instrument interacts with the circuit under test. An ideal voltmeter has infinite input resistance and an ideal ammeter has zero series resistance; real instruments are not ideal.

- Connect a voltmeter in parallel with the element and an ammeter in series.
- Choose a range above the expected value, then reduce the range if appropriate.
- Observe polarity, units, probe attenuation, frequency limits, and instrument ratings.
- Measurement loading, noise, calibration, resolution, and uncertainty can affect results.

## 2. Digital voltmeter and ramp techniques

A digital voltmeter (DVM) conditions an input, converts its value to a digital representation, and displays the result. A typical instrument includes input protection, range selection/attenuation, an analog-to-digital converter, reference, control logic, and display.

### Single-ramp time conversion

A ramp voltage with known slope is compared with the unknown input. The time interval required for the ramp to reach the input level is measured with a clock. If the ramp slope is $S=dV/dt$:

$$
\Delta t=\frac{V_{in}}{S}
$$

If the counter clock is $f_{clk}$, the count is approximately:

$$
N=f_{clk}\Delta t
$$

Single-ramp conversion depends on ramp slope and clock accuracy, and is sensitive to errors in the ramp and timing circuitry.

### Dual-slope integration

Many integrating DVMs use two phases:

1. Integrate the unknown input for a fixed time $T_1$.
2. Apply a known reference of opposite polarity and measure the time $T_2$ needed to return the integrator output to zero.

For an ideal integrator with the same $R$ and $C$ in both phases:

$$
V_{in}T_1=V_{ref}T_2,\qquad
V_{in}=V_{ref}\frac{T_2}{T_1}
$$

The integration components cancel in the ratio. Averaging over a chosen integration interval also rejects some periodic interference, but conversion is relatively slow.

### Example: dual-slope conversion

A DVM integrates the unknown for $T_1=100$ ms. It then uses a 1.0 V reference and measures $T_2=75$ ms:

$$
V_{in}=1.0\frac{75}{100}=0.75\ \text{V}
$$

## 3. Digital multimeters

A digital multimeter (DMM) combines voltage, current, and resistance measurement, often with continuity, diode-test, capacitance, and frequency functions. The internal method depends on the measurement and instrument design.

- **DC voltage:** A high-input-resistance circuit measures potential difference.
- **AC voltage:** The meter may measure average-responding or true-RMS values; check the specified waveform and frequency range.
- **Current:** The meter inserts a shunt or current-sense element in series. Use the correct current terminal and fuse.
- **Resistance:** The meter applies a small test current or voltage and calculates resistance. Never measure resistance in an energized circuit.
- **Continuity/diode test:** Applies a test signal and reports conduction or forward drop; the meter's test current/voltage is limited.

Resolution is the smallest displayed increment, not a guarantee of accuracy. Accuracy specifications often include a percentage of reading plus a number of counts. Do not exceed the meter's voltage, current, category, or input-terminal ratings.

### Example: DMM accuracy

A meter specifies DC accuracy of $\pm(0.5\%\text{ of reading}+2\text{ counts})$. On a 20.00 V range with 0.01 V resolution, a reading of 10.00 V has a bound:

$$
\pm\left(0.005(10.00)+2(0.01)\right)
=\pm0.07\ \text{V}
$$

This estimate follows the stated specification; actual uncertainty may include calibration, environment, and measurement setup.

## 4. Cathode-ray oscilloscope (CRO)

An analog CRO displays input voltage as a vertical deflection against time represented by horizontal sweep.

### CRT principle

A cathode-ray tube (CRT) contains an electron gun, focusing and accelerating elements, deflection plates, and a phosphor screen. The electron beam produces a visible spot. Vertical deflection is proportional to input voltage; horizontal deflection is generated by a time-base sweep.

### Simplified oscilloscope signal path

1. **Probe/input coupling and attenuator:** Scales the signal and sets AC/DC coupling.
2. **Vertical amplifier:** Drives vertical deflection and sets volts per division.
3. **Trigger/synchronization:** Starts the sweep at a consistent point on the signal.
4. **Time base/horizontal amplifier:** Sweeps the beam across the screen at a selected time per division.
5. **Display:** Shows the resulting waveform.

### Voltage measurement

Count vertical divisions from the reference level to the waveform:

$$
V_{pp}=N_{div}\times(\text{V/div})\times(\text{probe factor})
$$

The probe factor is often 1x or 10x. The oscilloscope's channel setting must match the probe.

### Time and frequency

$$
T=N_{div}\times(\text{s/div}),\qquad f=\frac{1}{T}
$$

### Phase difference

For two same-frequency signals, measure horizontal separation $\Delta t$ between corresponding points:

$$
\phi=360^\circ\frac{\Delta t}{T}
$$

The sign (lead or lag) depends on which waveform reaches the reference point first.

### Current measurement

A scope measures voltage, not current directly. Insert a known shunt resistor $R_s$ in series and measure its voltage:

$$
I=\frac{V_{R_s}}{R_s}
$$

The shunt must not significantly disturb the circuit, and the oscilloscope ground connection must be safe for that circuit. Use a proper current probe where appropriate.

### Example: reading a waveform

A sine wave spans 4 vertical divisions on a 2 V/div setting with a 10x probe. Its peak-to-peak voltage is:

$$
V_{pp}=4(2)(10)=80\ \text{V}
$$

If one cycle spans 5 horizontal divisions at 0.2 ms/div:

$$
T=5(0.2\ \text{ms})=1.0\ \text{ms},\qquad f=1.0\ \text{kHz}
$$

For two signals with $\Delta t=0.25$ ms at this frequency:

$$
\phi=360^\circ\frac{0.25}{1.0}=90^\circ
$$

## 5. Digital storage oscilloscope (DSO)

A DSO samples the input with an analog front end and analog-to-digital converter (ADC), stores samples in memory, and reconstructs a waveform on the display. Triggering and acquisition settings determine which data are captured.

Important terms:

- **Sample rate:** Samples per second.
- **Record length:** Number of stored samples per acquisition.
- **Bandwidth:** Frequency range over which the front end responds within its specification.
- **Trigger:** Event that stabilizes or selects an acquisition.
- **Aliasing:** A sampled display can misrepresent a signal if sampling is insufficient or acquisition settings are unsuitable.

For a record of $N$ samples acquired at sample rate $f_s$, the record duration is:

$$
T_{record}=\frac{N}{f_s}
$$

The Nyquist criterion says a band-limited sinusoid must be sampled above twice its highest frequency to avoid fundamental aliasing, but practical waveform shape measurement requires substantially more samples per cycle and adequate analog bandwidth.

### Analog scope and DSO comparison

| Feature | Analog CRO | DSO |
|---|---|---|
| Signal display | Continuous beam deflection | Digitized samples reconstructed on screen |
| Storage | Usually requires camera/storage option | Waveforms can be stored, recalled, and analyzed |
| Single-shot events | More limited | Often captures and stores one-time events |
| Acquisition concerns | Trigger and display persistence | Sample rate, record length, aliasing, interpolation, and trigger |
| Measurements | Primarily visual/graticule | Visual plus automatic measurements and processing |

Digital storage is powerful but does not guarantee a correct display; verify sample rate, bandwidth, probe setup, and trigger conditions.

### Example: sample spacing

A DSO samples at $100$ MS/s while capturing a $1$ ms record. The approximate sample interval and sample count are:

$$
\Delta t=\frac{1}{100\times10^6}=10\ \text{ns}
$$

$$
N=f_sT=(100\times10^6)(1\times10^{-3})=100\,000\ \text{samples}
$$
